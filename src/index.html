    Help

    How it works

    The sensor is a single ultrasonic transducer that both sends and receives, from the same spot. It sends a short burst (a "ping"), then switches to listening. The sound spreads out in a beam, bounces off surfaces and gets weaker with distance, in the air, and at every bounce. The sensor takes the first returning sound louder than its threshold as the echo and turns the echo time into a distance:

    distance = c × t / 2

    where c is the speed of sound the sensor assumes (343 m/s, i.e. 20 °C) and t is the time from ping to echo. The sound travels there and back, hence the divide by 2.

    Try this

    • Press Ping (or Space) and watch the pulse spread, bounce and come back.
    • Select the wall and rotate it a few degrees at a time: once the mirror-like bounce misses the sensor, the echo drops out.
    • Change the wall's material to acoustic foam: the echo sinks below the threshold.
    • Change the temperature: the real echo time changes, but the sensor still assumes 20 °C, so the reading drifts.
    • Load Crosstalk: two sensors, turn on Loop and open Distance history: false echoes from Sensor B creep closer every ping.
    • Turn on Intensity slice to see where the sound actually goes.

    Model and limits

    At 40–60 kHz the wavelength is only a few millimetres, much smaller than walls and pipes, so sound is traced as rays (like light). Diffraction around edges and phase interference between overlapping echoes are not modelled. Levels are in dB SPL at the sensor.

    Transform

    Position (m)
    Where the transducer face is. X and Z are across the floor, Y is height above it. Sending and receiving happen at this one point.
    Aim: yaw, pitch (°)
    Yaw turns the sensor left/right around the vertical axis (0° points along +X). Pitch tilts it up (+) or down (−); −90° points straight at the floor. You can also aim with the rotate tool (E).

    Transducer

    Model
    Presets that fill in frequency, bandwidth and beam angle. Changing any of those switches the model to Custom.
    Frequency (kHz)
    The operating frequency. Higher frequencies give a shorter wavelength and finer detail, but the air absorbs them faster: about 1.3 dB/m at 40 kHz and 1.9 dB/m at 58 kHz (20 °C, 50 % humidity). Echoes are at this frequency.
    Bandwidth (kHz)
    Width of the receiver's filter, centred on the frequency (−3 dB at frequency ± bandwidth/2). It decides how much of other emitters gets through; a narrow band rejects neighbours on nearby frequencies.
    Beam angle (°)
    Half-angle of the beam where the level has dropped by 6 dB (the drawn cone). The beam is modelled as a circular piston: weaker side lobes extend beyond the cone. A narrow beam ignores clutter but misses off-axis objects; a wide beam sees more, including the floor.
    Tx level (dB SPL)
    How loud the ping is, measured on the beam axis 1 m away. Raising it makes every echo louder by the same amount while the noise floor (20 dB SPL) and the threshold stay put, so the range grows.
    Pulse (cycles)
    Length of the burst in periods of the frequency; duration = cycles ÷ frequency (8 cycles at 58 kHz ≈ 0.14 ms). Longer pulses carry more energy but smear close echoes together.

    Receiver

    Threshold (dB SPL)
    The level a received signal must reach to count as an echo. The first crossing after the blanking time is the measurement. Lower = more range, but more false triggers from the floor, clutter and other emitters.
    Blanking (ms)
    Because the same transducer sends and receives, it keeps ringing for a moment after the ping and can't hear anything yet. The receiver ignores this time. It sets the minimum range ≈ c × blanking ÷ 2 (1.2 ms ≈ 0.21 m); objects closer than that are not seen.
    Max range (m)
    How long the sensor listens: echoes arriving after 2 × range ÷ c are ignored. It also sets the length of the echo chart.
    Ping interval (ms)
    Time between pings when looping. Matters for crosstalk: when another sensor uses a different interval, its pulses land at a different time in every ping.
    Ping offset (ms)
    When this sensor fires relative to the other sensors. Staggering sensors by more than the longest echo time keeps them from hearing each other.

    Shape

    Wall / Block
    Boxes with width (W), height (H) and depth (D) in metres. Flat faces act like mirrors.
    Pipe
    A cylinder with a radius (minimum 3 cm) and length, upright by default; rotate it to lie down. A curved surface spreads the echo, so pipes return much less than a wall at the same distance, but from a wide range of angles.
    Sphere
    A ball with a radius. Curved in both directions, so it spreads the echo even more.
    Position / Rotation
    Centre of the object (m) and its rotation around X, Y, Z (°). Or use the move (W), rotate (E) and resize (R) tools.

    Surface

    Material
    Presets for absorption and scattering at ultrasonic frequencies. Editing either slider switches to Custom.
    Absorption (0–1)
    Share of the sound energy lost at each bounce. Hard surfaces (concrete, steel, glass, water) are 0.01–0.06; acoustic foam ≈ 0.85, so almost nothing comes back.
    Scattering (0–1)
    Share of the reflected sound that is spread in all directions instead of bouncing like a mirror. 0 = perfectly smooth: a tilted wall sends the echo away and the sensor hears nothing. Rough surfaces (brick) scatter some sound back from any angle, so they are seen even when tilted, but more weakly.

    Floor

    The floor is always there and is as large as the room size (Environment tab). Click its eye icon in the Scene list to stop it reflecting. Beams that are wide or low often catch the floor, which shows up as an early false echo.

    Interference sources are other things making ultrasound. Every other sensor in the scene is also a source for the active sensor.

    Type
    Other ultrasonic sensor: short bursts at its frequency. Broadband hiss: continuous noise spread over 20–100 kHz, like a compressed-air leak; the sensor only hears the slice inside its band. Continuous tone: a steady whistle at one frequency.
    Frequency (kHz)
    Compared with the active sensor's band. The box under the settings shows how much gets through the filter: a 57 kHz neighbour passes almost fully to a 58 ± 2 kHz sensor, a 40 kHz one is cut by about 38 dB.
    Level (dB SPL)
    How loud it is on its axis at 1 m.
    Beam angle (°)
    Half-angle at −6 dB, as for sensors. 90° = omnidirectional (same level in every direction).
    Mode
    Sensor-type sources can be Pulsed (bursts) or Continuous.
    Period (ms)
    Time between its bursts.
    Start offset (ms)
    When its first burst happens, measured from the active sensor's first ping.

    Why false echoes move

    If a source fires every 55 ms and your sensor every 60 ms, each ping hears the source 5 ms earlier than the last, so the false distance creeps closer ping after ping. Turn on Loop and watch Distance history. Giving sensors the same interval and offsets further apart than the echo time avoids it.

    Air

    Temperature (°C)
    Sets the real speed of sound: c = 331.3 × √(1 + T/273.15) m/s (343.2 m/s at 20 °C). The sensor always assumes 20 °C, so at other temperatures its reading is off by about 0.17 % per °C.
    Humidity (%)
    Changes how strongly the air absorbs ultrasound (ISO 9613-1). The effect is large at these frequencies.
    Pressure (kPa)
    Also enters the air-absorption formula. Sea level is 101.3 kPa.

    Derived

    Speed of sound / Sensor assumes
    The real value and the one the sensor uses for its distance calculation.
    Wavelength
    c ÷ frequency, about 6 mm at 58 kHz. Everything much larger than this reflects like a mirror.
    Air absorption (dB/m)
    Extra loss per metre on top of spreading. An echo from 5 m travels 10 m, so it loses about 19 dB to the air at 58 kHz.

    Room

    Enclose in room
    Adds concrete walls and a ceiling, so sound keeps bouncing (reverberation) and late echoes appear.
    Room size (m)
    Width, height and depth. Also sets the size of the floor, even when the room isn't enclosed.

    Ray tracing

    Rays per ping
    How many rays leave the sensor. More rays give smoother, more reliable results but take longer; the time of the last trace is shown under the settings.
    Max bounces
    How many reflections a ray is followed through. Raise it for enclosed rooms and corner reflectors.
    Energy cutoff (dB)
    A ray is dropped once it is this much weaker than the ping at 1 m (spreading, air and bounces included). Lower = more faint late echoes, slower.
    Receiver radius (cm)
    The size of the transducer's receiving face. Each ray is treated as a thin beam, and this is the smallest width used when deciding how much of it the sensor catches. Around 0.5–1.5 cm is typical; larger values smooth the result but blur fine geometry.

    Intensity slice

    Show slice plane
    A heat map of all the sound passing through a plane during one ping, every bounce included.
    Plane
    Along the beam: an upright cut through the sensor's axis, great for seeing the beam, side lobes and floor. Horizontal: a cut parallel to the floor at the chosen height.
    Include other emitters
    Adds the sound from interference sources and other sensors.
    Colours
    Run from the plane's strongest spots (top of the slice legend) down 40 dB. Weaker areas are more transparent.

    Visualization

    Particles shown
    How many rays are drawn as moving wavefront dots. Doesn't change the result.
    Wavefront range (dB)
    Only rays launched within this many dB of the beam axis are drawn. About 6 dB matches the cone; 40 dB or more also shows the weak side lobes (the dome shape). Drawing only.
    Colour by
    Intensity: level on the legend scale. Bounces: a colour per reflection count. Source: blue for the sensor, orange for other emitters.

    Echo (A-scan) chart

    Blue trace
    What the sensor receives from its own ping (plus background noise), in dB SPL over time. The spike at the start is the ping itself ringing down.
    Orange trace
    Sound from other emitters (interference) after the receiver's filter.
    Red dashed line
    The threshold. The red marker is where the sensor detected its echo.
    Green dashed line
    Where the true echo from the object straight ahead would be.
    Grey areas
    Left: the blanking time. Right: beyond max range. Nothing is detected there.
    Axes
    Bottom: time in ms. Top: the distance the sensor would report for that time. Click or drag on the chart to scrub time.

    Readouts

    True distance
    Distance to the first surface straight along the sensor's axis.
    Measured / Error
    What the sensor reports, and the difference. Readings are usually a few cm short because the echo is detected on its rising edge, as real sensors do.
    Status
    OK: within 5 cm or 3 %. Off target: the first echo came from something else, or from a point off the axis. No echo: nothing crossed the threshold. False echo: another emitter triggered it. Echo, nothing ahead: nothing on the axis, but something else answered.

    Other tabs

    Ray log
    Every echo path, strongest first: arrival time, the distance it would read as, level, and the surfaces it bounced off. The highlighted row caused the detection.
    Distance history
    One point per finished ping (blue) against the true distance (green). Red ticks are pings with no echo.
    Compare sensors
    Every sensor's result side by side. Click a row to make that sensor active.

    3D view

    Dots are the wavefront moving out; lines show paths that reached the sensor (orange = interference). The legend colours are dB relative to the ping on its axis at 1 m.
